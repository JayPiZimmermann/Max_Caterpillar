                                                              TWO ReLU LAYERS IS ALL YOU NEED

                                                         FLORESTAN BRUNCK, CHRISTOPH HERTRICH, KILIAN RUESS,
                                                                 JACK STADE, AND AMIR YEHUDAYOFF


                                                 Abstract. We prove that every ReLU network can be exactly simulated by a ReLU
                                                 network with two hidden layers. We present two proofs: one that is based on fillings in
                                                 homological algebra and a direct combinatorial proof.

                                                                                 1. Introduction

                                         The ubiquity and success of neural networks as a model of computation motivates studying
                                         the capabilities of their architectures. This study raises familiar complexity-theoretic
                                         questions, like what is the class of functions they can compute, at what depth, with how
                                         many gates, etc.?
                                         This work investigates the role of depth in neural networks. On the one hand, deep learning
                                         is one of the major successes of modern computer science. On the other hand, classical
                                         approximation theorems dating back to the late 1980s show that, for many activation
                                         functions including ReLU, a single hidden layer suffices to approximate any continuous
                                         function arbitrarily well on a compact domain [10, 19, 24]. Although various explanations
                                         for the power of compositions have been proposed [5, 26], a quantitative understanding of
                                         the role of depth remains incomplete [20, 17].
                                         The role of depth has been studied from several perspectives. One line of work investigates
                                         depth-width tradeoffs in expressivity: can additional layers reduce the resources needed to
                                         represent or approximate a function [2, 12, 32]? Another asks whether depth can provide
                                         advantages for training [29]. Perhaps the most fundamental question, however, is whether
                                         deeper networks can represent functions that shallower networks cannot. Until now, this
                                         question has remained open.
                                         We focus on neural networks that use the ReLU(x) = max{0, x} activation function, which
                                         are among the most widely used ones in practice [13, 20]. A ReLU network with k hidden
                                         layers (and depth k + 1) is traditionally defined as the alternating composition
                                                             T (k) ◦ ReLU ◦T (k−1) ◦ · · · ◦ ReLU ◦T (1) ◦ ReLU ◦T (0)
                                         where T (i) is an affine map between two Euclidean spaces and ReLU stands for entry-wise
                                         application.
                                         ReLU networks compute continuous and piecewise linear (CPWL) functions. These are
                                         continuous functions that are affine on each cell of a finite polyhedral partition of the input
                                         Euclidean space. Conversely, every CPWL function can be computed by a ReLU network
                                         (see e.g. [2]). Their ability to capture nonlinear behavior while retaining a simple local
                                         affine structure makes CPWL functions useful beyond neural networks. For example, they
                                         are central to finite element methods [16], and their structure enables exact verification of
                                         stability conditions in robotic control [11].

be computed by less than ⌈log2 (n)⌉ hidden layers.

and Loho [14] proved an Ω(log log n) lower bound for ReLU networks that “respect the

1.2. Overview of the Homological Algebra Implementation. Let us start with some
intuition. Imagine we already found a two-layer representation of ∆n . By restricting the
representation to a facet, we get a two-layer representation of ∆n−1 . Each facet has its
own representation and the n + 1 representations are somehow glued together coherently.
What extra conditions does the boundary representation need to satisfy in order for it to
extend to the n-simplex?
This type of “boundary filling” question naturally suggests using the framework of chain
complexes and homology. A chain complex C• is a sequence of vector spaces C0 , C1 , C2 , . . .
equipped with linear boundary maps d : Ck → Ck−1 . The boundary map satisfies d2 = 0,
which implies that the image of d : Ck+1 → Ck is a subspace of the kernel of d : Ck → Ck−1 .
This leads to homology:
                   Hk (C• ) = ker(d : Ck → Ck−1 )⧸img (d : C            .
                                                             k+1 → Ck )
An element x satisfying d(x) = 0 is called a cycle (we use lowercase x to distinguish a
formal element x of a chain complex from a polytope, which we would represent with
a capital X). An element x that is equal to d(y) for some y is called a boundary. The
condition that d2 = 0 says that every boundary is a cycle. The homology groups record
which cycles are boundaries.
In Section 2, we define the chain complex P• of formal Minkowski differences of polytopes
modulo lower dimensional polytopes. This is the main chain complex we care about,
because it corresponds to ReLU networks by the duality discussed above. A central
property for performing induction is that a top-dimensional class in P• is determined by
its boundary. Namely, for chains of polytopes in Rn , the map d : Pn → Pn−1 is injective.
This is a consequence of known results (see Theorem 3). So if we can find some x ∈ Pn
such that d(x) = d(∆n ) then we actually obtained ∆n .
We let Λ• be the subcomplex of P• generated by paramids. This chain complex corresponds
to the subclass of two-layer ReLU networks we focus on. The homology of Λ• tells us a
lot about the functions that can be represented in terms of paramids. If we can represent
the n-simplex by paramids, then the boundary of the (n + 1)-simplex is a cycle in Λn .
By injectivity, the question of whether we can represent the (n + 1)-simplex by paramids
exactly asks whether this cycle is a boundary in Λ• .
The Minkowski sum relations in Λ• are complicated, and it is difficult to perform calculations
in this complex directly. So we try to approximate Λ• with a chain complex that is easier
to understand. We define a chain complex C• of parallelepipeds. There is a coning map
ρ : Ck → Pk+1 that sends a parallelepiped X to the paramid with base X and apex at
                                                   5
the origin. The image of ρ lies in the subcomplex Λ• of P• . A parallelepiped is a sum of
intervals so it is zero in Pk for k ≥ 2. The parallelepiped facet of a k-dimensional paramid
is zero in Pk−1 for k ≥ 3. Because the base does not survive in Λ• , this means that ρ
commutes with the boundary maps of C• and P• for k ≥ 2.
Let us try to implement the inductive construction. We start with a chain of parallelepipeds
x ∈ Cn−1 such that ρ(x) is the n-simplex. We then create copies of x for each facet of
the (n + 1)-simplex to get a cycle x′ ∈ Cn−1 such that ρ(x′ ) is the boundary of the n + 1
simplex. If we can find some y ∈ Cn such that d(y) = x′ , then ρ(y) satisfies
                           d(ρ(y)) = ρ(d(y)) = ρ(x′ ) = d(∆n+1 ).
Injectivity tells us that ρ(y) is the needed (n + 1)-simplex.
This still does not explain how we find y ∈ Cn with d(y) = x′ . We notice that not all cycles
in C• are boundaries. Every boundary x in C• satisfies the augmentation condition, which
says that x becomes 0 if we work modulo translations, because pairs of opposite faces of a
parallelepiped give sign-reversed translates of each other after taking the boundary. But
there are cycles in C• that do not have this property, see for example Figure 3.

We show that this is the only obstruction to filling boundaries in C• : any cycle with
the augmentation condition is a boundary. The idea is that non-trivial homology in C•
comes from relations among edge directions (see Section 3.1, or Section 5.2 for a different
perspective). For example, the triangle illustrated in Figure 3 is a cycle in C1 that is not a

We first illustrate our solution with the example of a regular hexagon, as shown in Figure 4.
Of the three edge directions t1 , t2 and t3 , we choose the free generating set t1 and t2 . The
two edges with direction t3 can be connected by parallelepipeds, as shown in the right
part of the figure. After subtracting the boundary of these parallelepipeds, the remaining
boundary (bottom part of the figure) has only t1 and t2 as edge directions. Since t1 and t2
are independent, this remaining boundary can be filled.
More generally, choose a set E of edge directions that have no relations among them. Let
x ∈ Ck be a cycle with the augmentation property. Write X = x0 + · · · + xk , where xi is
a parallelepiped with i edge directions in E and k − i edge directions not in E. Letting

subtracting y from x, we reduce the number of non-zero terms. Inductively, this implies

                        2. The Chain Complex of Polytopes

We start by defining a chain complex P•n . Here Pkn should represent “k-dimensional
polytopes in Qn under Minkowski sum, modulo polytopes of dimension < k”.
For a finite-dimensional real vector space S, let VS be the vector space of formal Minkowski-
linear combinations of polytopes. Formally, VS is the Q-vector space spanned by pairs
(X, Y ) of polytopes, modulo the relation (X, X) = 0. Addition in VS is given by Minkowski
sum (X1 , Y1 ) + (X2 , Y2 ) = (X1 + X2 , Y1 + Y2 ). Multiplication by a constant is given by
λ(X, Y ) = (λX, λY ) when λ is positive, and λ(X, Y ) = (|λ|Y, |λ|X) when λ is negative.
If S ′ is a subspace of S, then there is a natural inclusion VS ′ → VS . We define VeS to be
the quotient of VS by the sum over all proper subspaces S ′ of S of VS ′ .
Formally, for k ≥ 1, we define Pkn to be the direct sum of VeS over each oriented k-
dimensional subspace S of Qn , with relations that reversing the orientation of a subspace
corresponds to reversing sign in VS .
P0n will be mostly irrelevant for our calculations, but we define it here for completeness.
For k = 0, P0n is defined as a direct sum over Qn -indexed copies of Q, modulo global
translations. Note a translation induces aLpermutation of the summands. Here we are
adding relations x − t(x) = 0, where x ∈ Qn Q and t is the linear map induced by the
translation t permuting the factors.
We want to define the boundary d : Pk+1    n   → Pkn . We start with k ≥ 1. Now Pk+1 is
generated (as a Q-vector space) by classes of form (X, 0) with X a polytope in a k + 1-
dimensional oriented subspace S ⊆ Qn . The boundary of (X, 0) is defined as the sum
over all unit vectors v ∈ S of the face of X in direction v, translated so that it lies in the
subspace U orthogonal to v. The orientation A of U is chosen so that v ∧ A represents the
orientation of S. Note that this term is zero in Pkn unless v is orthogonal to a k-dimensional
face of X, so only finitely many terms are non-zero.
When k = 1, P1n is generated by classes of form (X, 0), with X a line segment in a
1-dimensional space S. Let a and b be the endpoints of X, chosen so that the vector a − b
induces the orientation on S. We set d((X, 0)) to be theLclass of ea − eb , where ea is the
standard basis vector corresponding the a-component of Qn Q, and eb is the one of the b
component.

                                  5. Additional Ideas

This section contains some results and alternate proofs that did not fit into the main
body of this part of the paper. We believe that these ideas can be valuable intuition for
understanding our result.

5.1. The Universality of Paramids. Haase, Hertrich, and Loho show that ReLUn,2
is generated by support functions of polytopes that are each the convex hull of two
zonotopes (see Theorem 8 in [15]). By Propositions 9 and 10 in [15] and Theorem 2, such
a polytope can be written in terms of polytopes of form conv(A ∪ B), where conv(A ∪ B)
is a join of A and B (i.e. A and B have disjoint affine hulls and their tangent spaces are
transverse). Working modulo lower-dimensional pieces, we can ignore pieces except those
with dim(A) + dim(B) = n − 1.
When A or B is a point, the piece we get is a paramid. The purpose of this section is to
show that, modulo lower-dimensional terms, every such piece is equivalent to a paramid.
This somewhat justifies the choice to focus on paramids with our proof.
We write α : Rn → Rn for the antipodal map. The key result is the following:
Lemma 14. Let A and B be polytopes in Rn (n ≥ 1) with disjoint affine hulls. Suppose
A can be written A1 + A2 , where A1 and A2 have transverse tangent spaces. Then in VeRn ,
the class of conv(A ∪ B) is equal to (−1)dim(A2 ) times the class of conv(A1 ∪ (B + α(A2 ))).

Proof. In this proof, we use the convention that a zero-dimensional point has one −1-
dimensional facet, which is the empty set. The Minkowski sum of any polytope with the
empty set is empty. The way we use the induction hypothesis, we will sometimes need a
version of the claim where A1 or B empty. We will check this case first.
If both A1 and B are empty, then both conv(A ∪ B) and conv(A1 ∪ (B + α(A2 ))) are
empty, so the claim is trivial. If A1 is empty and B is not, then conv(A ∪ B) = B and
conv(A1 ∪ (B + A2 )) = B + A2 . Since A2 and B have dimension < n, these both represent
0 in VeRn .
We also handle the case dim(A2 ) = 0 separately. In this case, A2 is a point, and it is clear
that conv(A1 ∪ (B + α(A2 ))) is just conv(A ∪ B) translated by −A2 .
Now suppose that A and B are non-empty and A2 has dimension at least 1. The proof is
by induction on n. When n = 1, the conditions of the lemma are impossible to satisfy, and
so the result is true trivially.
We want to use Theorem 4. Since A and B have disjoint affine hulls, the facets of
conv(A ∪ B) are of form conv(F ∪ B) or conv(A ∪ G), where F is a facet of A or G is a
facet of B.
Since A1 and A2 have transverse tangent spaces, the facets of A = A1 + A2 are of form
F1 + A2 or A1 + F2 , where F1 is a facet of A1 or F2 is a facet of A2 . So the facets of
conv(A ∪ B) are:
     • for each facet G of B, conv(A ∪ G),
     • for each facet F1 of A1 , conv((F1 + A2 ) ∪ B), and
                                             21
      • for each facet F2 or A2 , conv((A1 + F2 ) ∪ B).

Since A and B have transverse tangent spaces, B and A2 do as well. So by a similar
argument to the above, the facets of conv(A1 ∪ (B + α(A2 ))) are:

      • for each facet F1 of A1 , conv(F1 ∪ (B + α(A2 ))),
      • for each facet G of B, conv(A1 ∪ (G + α(A2 )))
      • for each facet F2 of A2 , conv(A1 ∪ (B + α(F2 )))

By the inductive hypothesis, the class (in VeS , where S contains A1 , G and A2 ) of conv(A1 ∪
(G + α(A2 ))) is equal to (−1)dim(A2 ) times the class conv(A ∪ G). Similarly, the class of
conv(F1 ∪ (B + α(A2 ))) is equivalent to (−1)dim(A2 ) times the class of conv((F1 + A2 ) ∪ B).
The dimension of F2 is one less than the dimension of A2 , so the class of conv(A1 ∪ (B +
α(F2 ))) is −(−1)dim(A2 ) times the class conv((A1 + F2 ) ∪ B).
We notice that the outward facing normal vector of conv(A1 ∪ (G + α(A2 ))) is the same as
the one for conv(A ∪ G), and similarly for conv(F1 ∪ (B + α(A2 ))) and conv((F1 + A2 ) ∪ B),
while conv(A1 ∪ (B + α(F2 ))) and conv((A1 + F2 ) ∪ B) have opposite normal vectors. By
Theorem 4, this completes the proof.                                                      □

In particular, if A and B are zonotopes, then the class of conv(A ∪ B) is, up to lower
dimensional terms, equal to (−1)dim(A) times the class of conv({0}, B + α(A)). The latter
polytope is a paramid.
So, up to lower dimensional terms, support functions of paramids generate ReLUn,2 . Of
course, this follows from Theorem 13 as well.
Remark. Even if polytopes A and B in Rn are equivalent modulo terms of dimension < n,
it is not true in general that pyramids on A and B in Rn+1 are equivalent modulo terms
of dimension < n + 1. So Theorem 14 is not so useful for inductive arguments.
